Upper Limb Rehabilitation Robots

Akso — a desktop upper-limb rehabilitation training robot for stroke patients. A 3D-printed single-DOF manipulator, an STM32/FreeRTOS embedded controller, quintic-polynomial trajectory planning with PID position control, and a CHAI3D virtual scene together deliver safe passive and resistance training.

Solidworks & 3D Printing FEA Structural Optimization STM32 · FreeRTOS CAN · FOC Motor Drive Trajectory Planning · PID CHAI3D Virtual Training

1Overview

A desktop robot that takes over part of the repetitive, high-intensity work of post-stroke upper-limb training.

Stroke is a leading cause of motor disability: most survivors are left with some degree of hemiparesis, and timely, repetitive training in the first months strongly influences recovery. Professional therapists, however, are in short supply, and one-to-one manual training is exhausting, monotonous, and hard to standardize.

Akso (named after the Greek goddess of health) is a desktop, single-degree-of-freedom upper-limb rehabilitation training robot designed for this scenario. The patient's forearm rests on a rotating arm and grips a handle; the robot then either drives the limb along planned trajectories (passive mode, for patients with little voluntary movement) or applies adjustable resistance against the patient's own motion (resistance mode, for later-stage strength training).

The project covers the full system stack: mechanical design and 3D printing, finite-element structural optimization, a real-time embedded control system, kinematic and dynamic modeling, trajectory planning and PID position control with Matlab/Simulink co-simulation, physical tracking experiments, and a Windows host application connected to a virtual training environment.

Akso in use: the forearm rests on the rotating arm while the screen shows the virtual training task.

2System Architecture

From the host PC to the patient's limb, three subsystems — host software, embedded controller, and mechanism — form one closed loop.

Plan The host PC sets the training task and target trajectory
Command A Bluetooth serial link sends targets to the STM32 controller
Drive The CAN bus delivers torque commands to the C620 FOC ESC
Train The M3508 gearmotor rotates the arm and guides the limb
Feedback Encoder and force signals close the loop and update the virtual scene

The MFC host program exchanges data with the CHAI3D virtual scene through shared memory and talks to the STM32 main controller over a wireless Bluetooth serial link, so no cable disturbs the moving arm. The controller runs FreeRTOS, computes the control law, and commands the C620 electronic speed controller over a 1 Mbps CAN bus; the ESC drives the M3508 motor with field-oriented control and streams rotor angle, speed, torque current, and temperature back in real time. A single-axis force sensor at the handle measures the human–robot interaction force.

3Mechanical Design & Structural Optimization

A 3D-printed single-bar arm rotating on a crossed roller bearing, verified and optimized with finite-element analysis.

Base and single-bar arm

Designed in Solidworks and 3D printed: the base in photosensitive resin (SLA), the arm in PLA (FDM, 100% infill). Sliding handle mounts let the effective arm length be adjusted to each patient's limb.

Crossed roller bearing

An RU85 crossed roller bearing carries both the axial load and radial disturbances in one compact, high-precision joint, while a machined flange D-coupling locks the motor shaft to the arm without slip.

Handle

A rubber grip with an inline single-axis force sensor; a free-spinning turntable between handle and arm keeps the wrist orientation unconstrained, protecting the elbow and wrist joints during rotation.

Safety design

Master power cut-off, ESC over-temperature and over-voltage protection, metal protection rods limiting rotation to about ±60°, and torque/speed clamps in both the host software and the firmware.

Since the cantilevered arm is the main load-bearing part, a static FEA was run in Solidworks Simulation with custom PLA material properties and a 100 N load applied at the handle regions. Stress concentrates where the arm meets the load-bearing ring, so an enlarged reinforcement ring was added to spread the contact area: the maximum stress dropped from 1.667×10⁷ to 1.395×10⁷ N/m², the tip displacement from 12.47 mm to 10.05 mm, and the minimum safety factor rose from 3.03 to 3.512.

4Embedded Control System

An STM32 real-time controller drives a RoboMaster gearmotor over CAN and reports back to the host over Bluetooth.

Main controller

DJI RoboMaster Development Board Type A (STM32F427IIH6 @ 180 MHz), configured with STM32CubeMX/HAL. FreeRTOS schedules the CAN, Bluetooth, and control tasks to meet the system's real-time, multi-threaded requirements.

Actuation

An M3508 P19 brushless DC gearmotor (≈19.2:1, rated 3 N·m / 469 rpm) with a C620 FOC ESC. Output force at the handle is kept within the 0–45 N range recommended for upper-limb training.

Communication

A 1 Mbps CAN bus carries current commands and encoder/torque/temperature feedback between controller and ESC; an HC-05 Bluetooth serial module (~10 m) links the controller to the PC without cables.

Sensing and power

The ESC-decoded encoder provides joint angle and speed; an HYLY-019 single-axis force sensor (20 kg rated) at the handle measures push–pull force. A 0–24 V adjustable DC supply powers the drive system.

5Modeling & Trajectory Planning

Kinematic, dynamic, and motor models provide the basis for smooth, safe motion planning.

Forward and inverse kinematics were derived with the Denavit–Hartenberg convention, and a Monte-Carlo simulation of the reachable end-points produced the fan-shaped planar workspace bounded by the ±60° protection rods (end-point radius up to 330 mm). The dynamics follow from the Euler–Lagrange equation: with the base frame on the rotation axis the model reduces to τ = Izzθ̈ + fθ̇, with the inertia computed analytically part-by-part and cross-checked against Solidworks mass properties. The DC motor was modeled from its electrical and mechanical equations plus the gearbox ratio and efficiency, recovering undocumented parameters from the mechanical time constant.

Trajectories are planned with quintic-polynomial blends so that position, velocity, and acceleration all stay continuous across an accelerate–cruise–decelerate profile: the arm sweeps 0→60° in 2.5 s with 0.5 s blend phases and a π/6 rad/s cruise speed. The full chain — trajectory generator, PID controller with voltage limiting, motor and gearbox model, and arm dynamics — was co-simulated in Matlab/Simulink and tracked the planned path with near-zero error.

6Position Control Experiments

The position control system was validated on the physical robot with step, quintic, and sinusoidal reference trajectories.

Following a 60° step, the arm settles in about 2.5 s with virtually no overshoot or steady-state error — the dynamic response is deliberately softened so that a sudden setpoint change can never jerk the patient's limb. Tracking the quintic-polynomial trajectory is close under no load; in loaded trials with different users the response lags more as resistance grows, but the torque margin is sufficient to carry the limb to the target along the planned path.

For passive training, a ±60° sinusoidal reference with a 10 s period swings the arm back and forth continuously. Even under load, the peak-to-peak amplitude of the actual motion matches the reference, confirming that the planned training motion is delivered effectively to limbs with different impairment levels.

7Host Software & Virtual Training

An MFC host application and a CHAI3D virtual scene make the training observable, configurable, and engaging.

The host program (Visual Studio + MFC) opens separate read and send threads for the Bluetooth serial link, giving full-duplex communication with the controller. It displays the joint angle in real time, logs data for the therapist, supports position reset and offline slider-based testing, and clamps every outgoing command to a safe amplitude. Communication between the host process and the virtual scene runs through shared memory implemented with memory-mapped files, one of the fastest inter-process communication methods on Windows.

The virtual environment, built with CHAI3D and OpenGL, presents a two-ball chasing game matched to the robot's circular motion: the patient steers a ball by moving the robot arm to catch a target ball, with an on-screen force-feedback bar and hotkeys for adjusting control gains. Clinical literature suggests such virtual-reality feedback improves engagement and training outcomes.
